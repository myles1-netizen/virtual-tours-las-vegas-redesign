When an agent sends me a listing that "just isn't performing," the photos almost always share the same three problems: blown-out windows, blurry detail, and walls that lean like they're about to fall over. All three are fixable. All three are what HDR real estate photography is for.

## Problem one: white windows

This is the classic cell-phone shot. The room looks fine but the window is a solid white rectangle — no view, no sky, no sense of what's outside. Buyers read a blown-out window as "there's nothing to see out there," even when the view is a pool or the Strip.

HDR fixes it. I take multiple exposures of the same frame — one bright enough to hold the detail outside the glass, one balanced for the room — and blend them into one image. The window shows the view. The room shows its true color. Clean.

## Problem two: soft, blurry detail

Blur usually comes from two places: a phone camera struggling in low light (it cranks the ISO and everything gets grainy), or a real camera handheld at a slow shutter speed. Either way, the photo looks muddy when you zoom in, and on a retina screen everything looks muddy now.

I shoot on a tripod at a low ISO with a longer exposure. The image is sharp corner to corner. You can read the model number on the oven. That sharpness reads as quality — and quality reads as "this home is worth the asking price."

## Problem three: crooked walls

This is the one buyers notice without knowing why. When a camera is tilted up or down, the vertical lines of the walls converge — the room looks like it's leaning. Kitchen cabinets look curved. Doorframes taper. It's subtle but it makes a listing feel off.

I shoot level, with the camera height set for each room, and correct any remaining convergence in editing. Walls stand straight. Cabinets sit flat. The space looks like it actually is.

## The light problem that's specific to Las Vegas

We get over 300 sunny days a year, and that desert sun is harsh. A south-facing room in Summerlin at noon can have a ten-stop difference between the window and the back wall. No single exposure can hold both. Without HDR, you pick — bright room, white window, or dark room, blown window. With HDR, you get both, every time.
